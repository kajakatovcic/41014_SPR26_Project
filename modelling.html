Modelling

State-space model, controllability and observability of the Waffle Pi

References to Tutorial

Unicycle and tracking-error models (Week 5 D), controllability (Week 5 D1), discretisation (Week 5 C5), the exact-arc discrete model and its Jacobian (Week 7 C.4), the 2-state cross-track model (Week 8 B). Code: src/control/model.py; evaluated by python tests/test_model.py.

1. Platform and kinematics

Waffle Pi: wheel radius \(r = 0.033\) m, track \(b = 0.288\) m, actuator limit 7.88 rad/s, encoders at 4096 ticks/rev. With wheel speeds \(\omega_l, \omega_r\):

\[ v = \frac{r(\omega_r + \omega_l)}{2}, \qquad \omega = \frac{r(\omega_r - \omega_l)}{b}. \]

A command that would exceed the usable wheel speed scales both wheels down together, which keeps the path curvature the controller asked for.

Modelling error found in the robot model itself

Before any model could match the robot, we found a bug in the ROBOTIS MuJoCo model: the chassis collision mesh includes the ball casters. Its convex hull touched the floor where the casters do, but with friction 1.0, and it carried 6.4 N of the robot's weight. MuJoCo also uses the larger friction of two touching geoms meaning the casters got the floor's friction too.

The effect: commanding 0.15 m/s gave 0.071 m/s, and the robot could not turn on the spot at all (0.001 rad/s for 0.5 commanded). The encoders overstated the motion so dead reckoning was also wrong. After giving the casters and chassis the casters' contact properties, with a higher contact priority, the encoders match ground truth: 0.117 against 0.116 m/s, and 0.281 against 0.279 rad/s.

2. Wheel actuator model - derived and measured

Each wheel is a MuJoCo velocity servo (torque \(= k_v(\omega_{cmd} - \omega)\)) with Coulomb joint friction and armature. Two parameters can be derived from the model file and both were then measured on the simulated robot:

QuantityDerivedMeasured
Deadband: each wheel turns this much slower than commanded and not below itfrictionloss / kv = 0.1 / 0.1 = 1.0 rad/s1.001–1.016 rad/s (mean 1.008)
Time constantJ / kv, J = 0.0110 kg m² (armature + wheel + m r²/2) → 0.110 s0.108 s
Turning resistance (identified NOT derived)–0.020 ± 0.001rad/s shortfall at every turn rate from 0.2 to 1.4 rad/s

The turning resistance is Coulomb-like. The same shortfall is seen whether the robot spins or arcs. It is most likely tyre scrub across the tread. The usable wheel speed is therefore 6.88rad/s. This is a top speed of 0.227m/s (not the 0.26m/s the actuator limit suggests) and 1.58rad/s turning on the spot. unicycle_to_wheels() compensates with +1.0rad/s per wheel and +0.02rad/s of turn rate in the direction of motion.

3. State-space models

Unicycle (Week 5 D), state \(\mathbf{x} = (x, y, \theta)\), input \(\mathbf{u} = (v, \omega)\):

\[ \dot x = v\cos\theta, \qquad \dot y = v \sin\theta, \qquad \dot\theta = \omega. \]

The EKF uses the exact-arc discrete form and its Jacobian \(F\) (Week 7 C.4). \(F\) matches a numerical Jacobian to \(1.4\times10^{-10}\).

Tracking error in the reference frame about a reference moving at \((v_r, \omega_r)\), with \(\mathbf{e} = (e_x, e_y, e_\theta)\):

\[ \dot e_x = v\cos e_\theta - v_r + \omega_r e_y, \qquad \dot e_y = v \sin e_\theta - \omega_r e_x, \qquad \dot e_\theta = \omega - \omega_r \]

Linearised about \(\mathbf{e} = 0\), with \(\delta\mathbf{u} = (v - v_r,\ \omega - \omega_r)\):

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \omega_r & 0 \\ -\omega_r & 0 & v_r \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The 2-state model the LQR tracker uses (Weeks 5 D, 8 B) is the \((e_y, e_\theta)\) block when speed is not controlled:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & v_r \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The \(\omega_r\) term enters as the curvature feed-forward \(\omega_r = v\kappa\). Sign convention: \(e = \) actual \(-\) reference (Week 8 writes \(\theta_d - \theta\)). The Jacobians were checked against sympy, and the error kinematics against the simulated unicycle.

The model has full rank whenever the reference moves and loses rank at rest. That is the nonholonomic constraint - the robot cannot correct a sideways error without driving. Slow tracking is only weakly controllable as controllability also weakens in proportion to speed. This appears again in D, where the cross-track time conststant scales with 1/v, and in docking, which cannot remove a lateral offset.

5. Observability of the pose

Method: local observability of the linearised time-varying system over 10 camera frames (1 s), \(\mathcal{O} = [W H_0;\ W H_1 F_0;\ \dots]\). It is weighted by the perception noise model (\(W = R^{-1/2}\)), so the singular values are in standard deviations per unit of state error. Test pose (−1.4, 0.2, 270°), with markers 1 and 3 in view. "moving" means (0.15 m/s, 0.2 rad/s).

  • Encoders observe nothing as they are inputs to the motion model, not measurements of the pose. This means dead reckoning drifts without bound.
  • One marker never makes the full pose observable even while moving. Rotating the robot and its whole path about the marker changes no range or bearing measurement. Heading becomes observable through motion with GPS fixes as discussed in week 8.
  • Two markers make the full pose observable whether seen at once or one after the other with odometry linking them.
  • Bearing carries far more information than range- singular values of about 7000 against about 430 for the same marker.

Over a room coverage map (0.1 m grid × 24 headings, 22,848 free poses), 60% of poses see no marker, 38% see one, and only 2% see two or more. The EKF therefore mostly corrects from markers seen one after another, with drift growing in between.

This result drove two design decisions. It is why there are wall landmarks besides the dock marker and, in the explore mission, it is why we added a LiDAR wall-line heading measurement. One marker plus the wall it hangs on observes the full pose.

6. Discretisation at T = 0.05sec (Week 5 C5)

  • 2-state model, vr = 0.15: zero-order hold gives Bd = [0.000187, 0.05]T; forward Euler gives [0, 0.05]T, missing the T²vr/2 term.
  • 3-state model, vr = 0.15, ωr = 0.4: zero-order hold keeps the open-loop eigenvalues on the unit circle (max |z| = 1.000000). Forward Euler pushes them outside (1.000200), an artificial instability for turning references. This is the reason a forward-Euler 3-state tracker was not used (D).
  • T = 0.05 s is under half the wheel time constant (0.11 s) and twice the camera rate.

8. Assumptions

  • Flat floor: no longitudinal wheel slip (holds after the caster fix, where the encoders equal ground truth). Slip and wheel-radius errors are then added deliberately for the estimator (C).
  • Wheel actuators: deadband, first-order lag and constant turning resistance. The lag is neglected in the control model. This is justified in D: the closed-loop time constants are 0.35 sec and 1.4–7 sec and every design settles within 0.15 sec of the linear prediction with the lag included.
  • Encoders quantised at 4096 ticks/rev; markers at known, fixed positions; camera rigidly mounted.
Beyond the tutorial content
  • An actuator model derived from the robot model's parameters and then confirmed by measurement.
  • A contact bug found and fixed in the supplied robot model with before-and-after numbers.
  • Observability weighted by the real measurement noise, which turns rank into information, plus a coverage map of where the camera can actually correct the filter.
  • The discretisation comparison used as evidence in a design decision (rejecting a forward-Euler tracker).
What these results do and do not support
  • The observability analysis is local (linearised along a trajectory). It shows which directions the sensors can see and not how quickly the EKF converges.
  • The coverage map uses geometry only (in view, within 3 m and 60°, not occluded). Measured detection rates are in B.
  • The actuator model is the simulated robot's. The real robot's firmware runs its own wheel speed loops so the deadband compensation is not used on hardware (Hardware).
